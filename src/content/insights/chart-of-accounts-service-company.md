A good chart of accounts makes your reports meaningful and your accountant's job quick. A messy one (fifty expense categories, "miscellaneous" everywhere) makes both useless. Here's a lean template for Nigerian service companies.

## What is a chart of accounts?

A **chart of accounts (COA)** is the structured list of accounts your transactions are recorded in. Every sale, expense, payment and tax lands in one of them, and your profit and loss and balance sheet are built from them.

Accounts are usually numbered by type:

| Range | Type |
|---|---|
| 1000–1999 | Assets |
| 2000–2999 | Liabilities |
| 3000–3999 | Equity |
| 4000–4999 | Income |
| 5000–5999 | Cost of sales |
| 6000–6999 | Operating expenses |

## Tips for keeping it useful

- **Fewer, clearer accounts.** 20–30 expense accounts is plenty for most SMEs.
- **Separate tax liabilities.** VAT, PAYE, pension and WHT each get their own account, so you always know what you owe.
- **Track WHT receivable** as an asset, since it's a tax credit you're owed.
- **Split cost of sales from overheads** so you can see gross margin.
- **Avoid "Miscellaneous".** If something recurs, give it an account.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a chart of accounts?

The organised list of accounts (assets, liabilities, equity, income and expenses) that a business records its transactions in.

### How many accounts should a small business have?

Enough to answer your key questions, usually 50–80 in total, with 20–30 expense accounts for a typical service company.

### Where do VAT and PAYE go in the chart of accounts?

As liabilities: separate accounts for output VAT payable, PAYE payable, pension payable and WHT payable.

### Is WHT deducted by clients an asset?

Yes. WHT deducted from your income is a tax credit, recorded as WHT receivable until you use it against your tax.
